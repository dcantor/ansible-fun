# Network Blog Launch Plan

## Purpose and identity

We are launching a cross-team network blog that publishes one post every two weeks. It gives engineers from every network team a place to share practical lessons. Success means most posts come from engineers, not managers.

**Mission (draft):** Real lessons from the people who keep us connected.

**Audiences, in priority order:**

1. Network engineers across all teams: shared techniques, standards and incident lessons
2. App, dev and infrastructure teams: how the network works and how to work with us
3. Leadership: visibility into the team's work and impact

**Name options:** Packet Notes · The Wire · Layer 8 · Network Field Notes · Hop by Hop

**Action items:**

- Pick a name (consider a quick vote across teams to build early buy-in)

## Team and roles

One team champion per network team spreads the work and keeps the blog from being seen as one manager's project.

| Role | Who | Responsibilities | Time commitment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Program owner | You | Sets direction, runs the monthly sync, recruits champions, recognizes contributors | About 2 hrs/week |
| Team champion (1 per team) | TBD | Spots story ideas in their team, nudges authors, reviews drafts from their area | About 1 hr/week |

## Content formats and templates

Short formats make contributing easy. Most first-time authors should start with a "Today I Learned" post or an interview.

| Format | Length | Template prompts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Today I Learned | 150–300 words | What did you learn? Where did it come up? The command or config that matters? |
| Incident story | 500–1,000 words | What broke? How did you find it? What fixed it? What changed afterward? |
| How we do X | 500–1,500 words | What is the practice? Why do we do it this way? Steps, with an example |
| Tool spotlight | 300–800 words | What problem does it solve? How do you use it? Where's the repo? Who built it? |
| Explain it for app teams | 400–800 words | The concept in plain words, a diagram, what they should do differently |
| Opinion or debate | 400–1,000 words | Your position, the evidence, the strongest counterargument |
| Interview | 20-min chat | An editor interviews the engineer, drafts the post, and the engineer reviews it and gets the byline |

**Every post includes:** a TL;DR at the top, at least one diagram, snippet or screenshot, and the author's name and team.

**Co-authoring is encouraged,** especially pairing a junior and a senior engineer.

**Action items:**

- Publish the templates in a shared space
- Write a one-page "How to contribute" guide

## Editorial process and cadence

We publish every other week and keep at least 3 posts ready in the pipeline at all times.

**How a post moves from idea to published:**

1. **Idea:** anyone adds it to the shared idea list, even if they won't write it.
2. **Claimed:** an author or interviewer takes it and sets a target date.
3. **Draft:** the author writes from a template; a champion is available to help.
4. **Edit:** an editor does a clarity pass within 3 business days. It's not a grammar test.
5. **Security check:** only for posts touching IP schemes, vulnerabilities or vendor issues.
6. **Scheduled:** the post goes into the next open slot.
7. **Published and promoted:** announced in channels and credited to the author.

**Rituals:**

- **Monthly editorial sync (30 min):** review the pipeline, claim topics, choose the next theme
- **Theme months (optional):** e.g. Automation, Security and Segmentation, Wireless, Cloud Networking
- **Ongoing series:** Network 101 (onboarding), Lessons from the Outage, Meet the Team

**Turning conversations into posts:** when a good Slack or Teams thread, ticket or incident comes up, a champion asks the person involved: "Want help turning this into a post?"

## Recognition and incentives

Recognition is the main reason people will contribute, so every post gets credited publicly and to the author's manager.

- **Shout-outs:** name authors in all-hands, team meetings and leadership updates
- **Manager visibility:** send a short note to the author's manager for each post
- **Career growth:** agree with the other network managers that posts count as evidence of communication and impact in reviews
- **Milestones:** a "First Post" badge, a quarterly "Most Useful Post" vote, small swag
- **Further opportunities:** turn strong posts into lunch-and-learns, onboarding material or conference talk proposals
- **Crediting the work:** name everyone who built the tool or worked the incident, not only the writer

**Action items:**

- Get the other network managers to agree on treating posts as review evidence

## Launch timeline

The plan takes 8 weeks: 4 to prepare, then a public launch, then 4 weeks to settle into the cadence. Weeks are counted from kickoff, so adjust them to your calendar.

**Phase 1: Foundation (weeks 1–2)**

- Confirm the platform and set up the blog space
- Recruit one champion per team and hold the first sync
- Choose the name and finalize the mission
- Agree on the security review rules with InfoSec

**Phase 2: Build the pipeline (weeks 3–4)**

- Publish the templates and the "How to contribute" guide
- Open the shared idea list and seed it with 15–20 topics
- Write the kickoff post (your "why")
- Personally ask 3 respected engineers for the first posts, offering interviews
- Have 4 posts ready before launch

**Phase 3: Launch (week 5)**

- Publish the kickoff post
- Announce at an all-hands or cross-team meeting
- Post the teaser and link in the main network and engineering channels

**Phase 4: Build momentum (weeks 6–8)**

- Publish posts 2–4 every other week
- Publicly recognize the first contributor who isn't a manager
- Send the first monthly roundup
- Hold a 30-day review: what's working, what to change

**Ongoing:** monthly sync, a post every other week, quarterly metrics review and recognition.

## Promotion and distribution

We take posts to where people already are instead of expecting them to visit the blog.

- **Channel teasers:** a 2–3 line summary and the link in network, engineering and app-team Slack or Teams channels
- **Monthly roundup:** a short digest sent to engineering and leadership
- **Onboarding:** link the Network 101 series in new-hire materials
- **Reuse:** link relevant posts in tickets, runbooks and incident reviews
- **Discussion:** ask a question at the end of each post to invite comments

## Success metrics

The main measure is how many different people contribute, not how many posts we publish.

| Metric | 6-month target | How we measure it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unique contributors | 10+ | Count of post bylines and co-authors |
| Teams represented | Every network team | Author team per post |
| Posts published | 12+ (every other week) | Blog archive |
| Readers per post | Set a baseline after month 1 | Page views from the platform |
| Reuse | 5+ links in tickets, runbooks or onboarding | Champions report it at the monthly sync |

The targets are starting points to adjust after the 30-day review.

## Risks and open decisions

The biggest risk is losing steam after launch, so a buffer of ready posts and active champions matter most.

| Risk | Mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| Momentum fades after the first few posts | Keep 3+ posts in the buffer; champions recruit authors; offer interviews |
| Only managers or senior engineers write | Promote short formats, co-authoring and interviews; personally invite junior engineers |
| Sensitive details get published | Agree on clear security review rules upfront; editors flag risky content |
| People don't have time to write | Keep posts short, let them write during work hours, give credit in reviews |
| Low readership | Post teasers in channels, send the monthly roundup, link posts in tickets and onboarding |

**Open decisions:**

- Which platform hosts the blog? (Confluence, presumably)
- Can posts be written during work hours, and how much time is OK?
- Should strong posts be shared outside the company, and who approves that?
- Final name and launch date
